# Style Profile: Firsthand AI Analysis

This profile abstracts a reusable editorial mechanism from representative AI writing. It does not authorize imitation of a living writer's distinctive expression.

## Reader promise

The reader is not only asking “what launched?” They want to know:

- whether the thing works outside the announcement;
- what it cost the writer to verify;
- which limitation the launch material hides;
- whether the event changes an existing workflow or industry assumption.

The draft should therefore combine the speed of news, the evidence of a product review, and the consequence of an industry essay.

## Title system

Prefer a concrete subject plus a consequence that exceeds the feature itself. Useful sources of tension include:

- an abnormal number or scale;
- an old system suddenly becoming cheap to attack or reproduce;
- a familiar product doing something outside its expected category;
- a company forced into a visible strategic reversal;
- a personal test producing a result that contradicts the launch narrative.

Use the strongest defensible claim. If the consequence is an inference, signal that with a question or conditional phrase instead of presenting it as settled fact.

## Opening

The first 100–250 Chinese characters should normally contain:

1. a concrete occurrence;
2. the writer's reason for opening, buying, testing, or tracing it;
3. the first sign that the result is not routine.

Avoid chronology that begins too early. Start where the situation becomes strange, then backfill history only when it helps explain the mechanism.

## Structural rhythm

Alternate four types of paragraph:

- **scene:** what the writer or source shows;
- **fact:** date, number, quote, version, or condition;
- **interpretation:** what the evidence suggests;
- **turn:** a failure, counterexample, hidden cost, or scale change.

Three interpretation paragraphs in a row usually signal abstraction. Add evidence or cut.

Product articles should not become feature inventories. Select only features that affect the article's central judgment. Industry articles should not become opinion columns; every escalation needs a fact anchor.

## Evidence and attribution

Give official sources priority for specifications and company claims, but label them as claims. Use direct testing to establish observable behavior, not universal performance. Use independent sources to challenge extrapolation.

Useful firsthand details include:

- exact entry point and version;
- price or account tier;
- the input and relevant constraints;
- wait time and retries;
- what was retained or lost;
- manual work required after generation;
- differences between expected and observed output.

Screenshots should support a nearby claim. A promotional hero image is context, not proof of performance.

## Voice

The writer can be curious, excited, skeptical, or annoyed, but the emotion must follow an action. Prefer “我付了100美元，第一轮就卡在……” over “这实在令人震惊.”

Allow opinions early. Readers should know what the writer currently thinks, while later evidence is allowed to revise that position. A visible change of mind is stronger than artificial certainty.

Colloquial language should make a mechanism easier to grasp. It should not replace the mechanism.

## Ending

Return to the specific test or anomaly and name the newly relevant decision rule. Strong endings commonly do one of the following:

- identify a cost that has moved from one participant to another;
- show which old professional boundary has weakened;
- explain what users should test before believing the next release;
- leave a precise unresolved question whose answer can be observed later.

## Failure modes

- **Fake participation:** inserting “我试了” without reproducible details.
- **Launch-note rewrite:** keeping every feature but never forming a judgment.
- **Premature grandeur:** declaring an era over before showing the anomalous result.
- **Slang cosplay:** adding memes and sentence fragments without lived evidence.
- **One-sided proof:** using the vendor's claims as both hypothesis and verification.
- **Caveat burial:** hiding material limitations in a final disclaimer after an absolute title.
